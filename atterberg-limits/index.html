Atterberg Limits Testing Across Longueuil's Sensitive Clay Zones

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

During a recent excavation for a mid-rise residential development adjacent to Chemin de Chambly, Longueuil geotechnical specialists encountered what they know well: a homogeneous grey silty clay deposit extending beyond six meters in depth, visibly saturated and sensitive to remolding. The contractor's immediate worry was bearing capacity, yet the deeper issue concerned how the soil's consistency would behave through alternating wet and dry construction phases. This underscores the critical role of Atterberg limits testing. By determining the liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index following ASTM D4318-17e1, we define the moisture thresholds that dictate soil behavior under loading. In Longueuil's post-glacial Champlain Sea soils, plasticity indices exceeding 25% are frequent, and neglecting these values can result in misclassifying excavation difficulty, shrink-swell potential, or even incorrect correlations for undrained shear strength. Our lab processes samples typically within 48 hours after extraction, guaranteeing that measured water contents represent in-situ conditions rather than drying-induced changes that would compromise the entire geotechnical model.

Plasticity index is not just a classification number — it governs the undrained shear strength ratio and the soil's sensitivity to disturbance in Longueuil's marine clays.

How we work

Comparing two sectors just a few kilometers apart illustrates why Atterberg limits demand local calibration. In the Vieux-Longueuil borough near the St. Lawrence River, the marine clay exhibits liquid limits frequently above 60% with plasticity indices in the 30-40% range, classifying as CH (high-plasticity clay) under the Unified Soil Classification System. These values correlate with significant volume change potential and low shear strength when remolded. Move inland toward the Saint-Hubert plateau, and the surficial geology shifts to a complex of till and reworked Champlain sediments where liquid limits often fall between 35% and 50%, with correspondingly lower plasticity. A single set of empirical correlations borrowed from Ontario or New England would misrepresent both conditions. Our approach integrates Atterberg determinations with the grain size analysis to confirm fines content and with the triaxial shear test when the project requires drained strength parameters for long-term stability analysis. This multi-test framework prevents the oversimplification that occurs when Atterberg results are interpreted in isolation, particularly in the transitional soil facies common across Longueuil.

Local geotechnical context

Longueuil sits within a region where the 1988 Saguenay earthquake (M5.9) and the broader Charlevoix seismic zone have shaped the engineering approach to soft soils. The city's population of approximately 250,000 occupies terrain underlain by Champlain Sea clays that can reach 60 meters in thickness along the river corridor. During cyclic loading, high-plasticity clays with PI exceeding 30% exhibit a different degradation pattern than low-plasticity silts, and the NBCC 2020 site classification — which feeds directly into the seismic design spectrum — relies in part on undrained shear strength parameters that are routinely correlated from Atterberg limits via relationships developed by Leroueil and others for eastern Canadian clays. A mischaracterized plasticity index leads to an incorrect site class, which cascades into either an overconservative or unconservative seismic design. Liquefaction assessment for silty zones near the Saint-Lawrence shoreline also depends on fines content and plasticity, since clays with PI > 15% are generally considered non-liquefiable under the Boulanger-Idriss (2014) framework, a distinction that directly impacts foundation type and cost.

Relevant standards

Relevant standards include ASTM D4318-17e1 for liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index; ASTM D2487-17e1 for soil classification under the Unified Soil Classification System; CSA A23.3-19 for concrete structure design referencing soil classification in foundation provisions per NBCC; and NBCC 2020 Part 4 for structural design requiring soil parameters for seismic site classification.

Questions and answers

What is the typical cost for Atterberg limits testing in Longueuil?

For a basic Atterberg limits suite (liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index) on one sample, the cost ranges from CA$100 to CA$130, contingent on whether wet preparation is needed due to organic content or high sensitivity. Multi-sample projects or combined classification packages receive reduced per-sample rates. This fee includes the certified laboratory report with the flow curve and USCS classification.

How do Atterberg limits relate to the sensitivity of Longueuil's marine clays?

The liquidity index, derived from the natural water content relative to the liquid and plastic limits, offers a direct measure of sensitivity. When LI is near or above 1.0, the in-situ water content meets or exceeds the liquid limit, indicating that the clay can become fluid-like upon remolding. This is typical of Champlain Sea 'quick clays' found in pockets across the Montérégie region and is essential for evaluating excavation stability and retrogression potential.

What sample quantity is required for Atterberg limits testing?

To perform the complete Atterberg suite, we need roughly 300 grams of material passing the No. 40 (425 µm) sieve. This is usually obtained from a 75 mm diameter Shelby tube sample or from a representative bulk sample of about 2 kg taken from a test pit. For highly sensitive clays, undisturbed tube samples are recommended to preserve in-situ water content, as drying during transport can alter measured limits and produce a misleadingly low plasticity index.
