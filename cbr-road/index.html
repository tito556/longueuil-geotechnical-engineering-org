Laboratory CBR Testing for Subgrade and Pavement Design in Longueuil

Beneath Longueuil’s surface, the Champlain Sea clay meets a dense glacial till layer that varies dramatically within just a few city blocks. From the redeveloped sectors near Place Charles-Le Moyne to the industrial corridors along Route 116, subgrade strength can shift from soft, sensitive marine silt to stiff, overconsolidated till. The laboratory CBR test provides a direct measurement of bearing capacity under controlled moisture and density conditions, an essential input when the difference between a durable pavement and premature rutting often comes down to how well the base course is characterized. Having processed hundreds of samples from Longueuil’s mixed geology, our team knows that remolding at field moisture content and running the penetration piston through a 4‑10” diameter specimen gives the most reliable design values. In projects near the Saint Lawrence River, where groundwater sits less than 2 m below grade, a conventional field test alone can miss the seasonal weakening that a soaked laboratory CBR test reveals. For deeper stratigraphic context, we often pair the CBR with SPT drilling to log refusal depths and identify where the till transitions into bedrock.

A soaked laboratory CBR test reveals the subgrade's weakest condition — the value you must design for, not the dry-season number.

How we work

Longueuil sits at roughly 15 m above sea level, but the soil profile beneath the city tells a story that goes much deeper — the Champlain Sea deposited up to 60 m of clay in some pockets, and the CBR values we measure in those fines can drop below 3% when saturated. The laboratory CBR test follows ASTM D1883 and AASHTO T-193, where a compacted specimen is soaked for 96 hours before a standard piston penetrates at 1.27 mm/min. In our experience across the South Shore, the soaked CBR is the make-or-break number: a subgrade that reads 12% at natural moisture can fall to 4% after four days of saturation, and that single shift changes the pavement structural number by a full tier. We measure both the 0.1-inch and 0.2-inch penetration values, reporting the higher of the two unless a correction is required, and we always run a companion moisture-density curve so engineers can see exactly where the soil lies relative to its optimum.

When the project involves granular base course specification, we complement the CBR with grain size analysis to confirm the percent passing No. 200 sieve, which directly affects drainage and frost susceptibility — a non-trivial detail in a climate where the frost penetration depth exceeds 1.5 m. The lab’s mechanical compactor is calibrated monthly against AASHTO T-180 energy levels, and every CBR mold is weighed before and after soaking to document water absorption, a proxy for the soil’s sensitivity to moisture changes that we’ve seen correlate strongly with post-winter pothole formation in Longueuil’s residential streets.

Local geotechnical context

Why relying on a single assumed CBR value in Vieux-Longueuil can be financially risky is clearly shown by the disparity between the dense till of the old center and the marine clays near Parc Michel-Chartrand. In the historic core, glacial till commonly offers CBR values exceeding 20%, and pavements designed back in the 1980s remain in good condition because the native soil forms a rigid base. Head eastward for two kilometers into the clay region, and the same pavement structure would break down after just three freeze-thaw cycles. The vulnerability of these clays becomes evident during the laboratory CBR test's soaking phase: when a remolded clay sample takes in 8 to 12% water by volume over 96 hours, its penetration resistance drops drastically, yielding a CBR of only 3 to 5%, which requires either a thicker granular base or chemical treatment. The 1998 ice storm highlighted this issue when saturated subgrades in Longueuil's low areas failed under emergency response vehicles, proving that extreme weather conditions exploit the weakest part of the pavement system. Even for commercial projects following MTQ standard drawings, we advise verifying the CBR at formation level because the transition zone between till and clay can be thinner than 50 cm—a distance easily missed by a grader operator but sufficient to cause differential settlement under repeated heavy loads.

Complementary services

01

Soaked CBR with Moisture-Density Relationship

The full AASHTO T-193 test method covers standard Proctor compaction, a 96-hour soak period, measurement of swell, and penetration testing at 0.05 inches per minute. The results provided include corrected CBR values at 0.1 and 0.2 inches, the percentage of moisture absorbed, and a plotted stress-penetration graph.

02

CBR with Companion Index Testing

We combine CBR testing with Atterberg limits and a grain size analysis on the same sample. This approach is especially valuable in Longueuil's clay areas, where the plasticity index and fines content explain the low CBR and inform stabilization strategies.

Questions and answers

How much does a laboratory CBR test cost in Longueuil?

A single-point soaked laboratory CBR test, which includes standard Proctor compaction and the required 96-hour soaking, typically costs between CA$170 and CA$290 per sample. The final price depends on whether a new moisture-density curve must be generated or if the optimum moisture content is already known from previous tests on the same material. For projects needing three or more points to establish a CBR-moisture relationship, we offer a reduced rate per specimen.

What is the difference between a field CBR and a laboratory CBR test?

A field CBR test employs a falling weight or penetration jack directly on the subgrade surface, providing a quick in-situ measurement, but it cannot control moisture levels. In contrast, the laboratory CBR test remolds the soil to a target density and soaks it for four days before penetration, simulating the worst-case scenario after seasonal wetting. In Longueuil's marine clays, the field CBR can be up to three times greater than the soaked lab value, which is why most municipal pavement designs mandate the laboratory result.

How long does it take to get CBR test results?

Standard turnaround time is five to seven business days. The 96-hour soaking period alone dictates much of this schedule, followed by penetration testing and then 24 hours for data analysis, curve corrections if needed, and report writing. If the project is on a tight timeline, we can run the moisture-density curve concurrently and provide a preliminary CBR value within 24 hours after the soak concludes.

Which CBR value should I use for pavement design — 0.1 inch or 0.2 inch?

Per AASHTO T-193, the higher of the two penetration values is typically used unless a correction is necessary for concave-upward curves. For Longueuil's fine-grained soils, the 0.2-inch value often comes out higher because the piston has passed through the remolded surface crust by that point. We report both numbers, note which one is controlling, and indicate the correction factor if applied.
