Seismic Microzonation Studies in Longueuil: Mapping Ground Response for Safer Construction

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

Longueuil sits directly atop the deep, sensitive clays of the Champlain Sea basin, a geological reality that shapes every major construction decision here. The Saint Lawrence River to the north and the Montérégie Hills to the south create a complex subsurface profile where seismic waves don’t behave uniformly. Site amplification can vary dramatically within a few blocks, which is why a city-wide seismic microzonation approach is essential. Our field experience shows that standard code spectra alone can underestimate design accelerations in the eastern plateau areas near Parc Michel-Chartrand. A proper microzonation campaign integrates ambient vibration measurements, borehole shear-wave velocity profiling, and geotechnical laboratory data to produce ground response maps that engineers can actually use. We combine this with MASW surveys to capture continuous Vs profiles, and where site access allows, we complement the work with seismic refraction to constrain bedrock depth across the municipality.

In Longueuil, the thickness of Champlain Sea clay can change by over 15 meters within a single city block, making site-specific ground response analysis indispensable for mid-rise and tall structures.

How we work

The contrast between the historic Vieux-Longueuil district and the newer residential developments in Saint-Hubert illustrates exactly why seismic microzonation needs localized calibration. In Old Longueuil, near-surface silty sands overlie the marine clay at shallow depths, resulting in a relatively stiff site class C or D in many locations. Just a few kilometers east in Saint-Hubert, the clay thickness increases substantially, pushing sites into class E territory with fundamental periods exceeding 0.5 seconds. These differences directly affect the design spectrum and the selection of foundation systems. Our field campaigns deploy downhole testing, MASW lines, and HVSR measurements to map these transitions with spatial resolution down to 250-meter grid spacing. The resulting Vs30 maps and amplification factor contours are then cross-validated against borehole data. When evaluating liquefaction potential in the sandy interbeds common near the riverfront, we incorporate findings from liquefaction assessments that follow NCEER methodology adapted for Eastern Canadian seismicity.

Local geotechnical context

For Longueuil, a city home to over 250,000 residents, the soil beneath key structures like the Jacques-Cartier Bridge approach and the Longueuil metro station is prone to seismic amplification. Although its epicenter was located more than 400 km away, the 1988 Saguenay earthquake generated unexpectedly strong shaking in the Saint Lawrence lowlands because the sedimentary basin trapped seismic waves. Through microzonation, it is possible to pinpoint which neighborhoods are most likely to experience spectral accelerations above the default code values. In the absence of such data, designs for schools, hospitals, and apartment buildings might unwittingly understate seismic loads by 20 to 30 percent. Given the existence of soft clay, irregular bedrock depths, and the city’s closeness to the active Western Quebec Zone, overlooking site effects would represent a genuine hazard for public safety and the city’s long-term resilience planning.

Complementary services

01

Field Seismic Characterization Campaign

To acquire shear-wave velocity profiles throughout the survey region, we use MASW arrays, downhole seismic cones, and HVSR single-station measurements. The survey layout considers the low Vs of Champlain clay and the requirement for adequate low-frequency resolution. Data collection adheres to ASTM D4428 and D5777 standards, and daily quality control checks are performed.

02

Site Response Analysis and Microzonation Mapping

From the measured Vs values and existing borehole logs, we conduct 1D equivalent-linear or non-linear site response analyses to produce amplification factors, design spectra, and contour maps of Vs30. Our deliverables comprise GIS-compatible layers, hazard curves for PGA and spectral ordinates, and a technical report that outlines the methodology and provides recommendations for structural engineers.

Relevant standards

The applicable codes and standards include NBCC 2020 (National Building Code of Canada, Part 4, Division B), CSA A23.3-19 (concrete design with seismic provisions), ASTM D4428/D5777 (crosshole and downhole seismic testing), the NEHRP site classification methodology (FEMA P-1050), and a comparative analysis using Eurocode 8 adapted for Eastern Canada's crustal seismicity.

Questions and answers

How much does a seismic microzonation study cost for a Longueuil project?

A seismic microzonation study for a standard Longueuil location costs between CA$5,320 (for a small single-building evaluation) and CA$24,830 (for multi-hectare projects or municipal mapping). The ultimate cost is contingent on grid density, necessary investigation depth, and how many field measurement points are required to account for the lateral variations in Champlain Sea clays across the area.

How long does a microzonation study take from start to finish?

Depending on the extent of the area and access limitations, a standard field campaign in Longueuil takes three to five working days. After that, three to four weeks are needed for lab data processing and site response analysis. Typically, the final report—which contains Vs30 maps and design spectra—is ready within six weeks from the project start, but larger municipal studies may have adjusted timelines.

Why can’t we just use the NBCC 2020 site class maps for Longueuil?

While NBCC 2020 supplies regional hazard data, it fails to reflect the detailed lateral variations in Longueuil’s Champlain Sea deposits. Across the city, clay depth, shear-wave velocity, and impedance contrasts vary quickly. By conducting a site-specific microzonation study, these local influences are captured, and it frequently uncovers amplification factors that substantially deviate from the broad code maps, particularly in transition areas between site classes D and E.

What site class does most of Longueuil fall into?

The past sedimentary processes of the Champlain Sea have made Longueuil's site classes extremely diverse. In central and eastern parts, most zones are classified as D or E, with Vs30 ranging from 150 to 350 m/s on average. Near the Montérégie Hills, closeness to bedrock outcrops shifts some western sections into class C, whereas thick clay deposits adjacent to the Saint Lawrence may nearly reach class F, necessitating site-specific ground response analysis.
